## Sprint resource review — 2026-09-26

### Evidence and source-access boundaries

- Josse emphasizes sprint skill, repeated manageable exposures, and integration
  with the rest of training. Stable volume can accompany improving execution and
  output. His frequency recommendations and CNS/recovery explanations are coaching
  judgments, not an individualized dose-response study or medical clearance.
- Clark, Rieger, Bruno & Stearne, *The National Football League Combine 40-yd
  Dash: How Important is Maximum Velocity?* DOI
  [10.1519/JSC.0000000000002081](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002081):
  [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28658072/) and
  [full-text transcription](https://www.scribd.com/document/1022774832/The-National-Football-League-Combine-40-Yd-Dash-11)
  reviewed, including methods and limitations; publisher PDF unavailable here.
  Online 2017, journal issue 2019. Retrospective modeling of 260 combine athletes,
  not a training intervention. Modeled maximum velocity related strongly to sprint
  times; the paper reports R² = .72/.83/.94 at 9.1/18.3/36.6 m. Limitations include
  pooled positions/body masses, timing-start effects, and population specificity.
  Association does not prove that adding flies improves acceleration. A clean PDF
  would help verify original figures/equations; text access was not abstract-only.
- Derek Hansen's [2014 high–low article](https://www.strengthpowerspeed.com/high-low-training-football/)
  was accessible through the browser's indexed text despite inconsistent live-site
  availability. It groups demanding modalities and protects recovery, but also
  acknowledges sport demands that cannot fit a strict high–low model. Treat this
  as a coaching framework, not a ban on all intermediate-intensity conditioning.
- Hansen's [2015 microdosing PDF](https://www.strengthpowerspeed.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/10/Micro-Dosing-with-Speed-and-Tempo-Derek-M.-Hansen.pdf)
  was accessible as indexed PDF text; programming guidance reviewed. It advocates
  small, high-quality exposures fitted around existing work, adequate rest, and
  reducing work when quality falls. Its injury-reduction claims are practitioner
  observations, not controlled evidence that a particular dose prevents injury.
- Full contents not accessed: Charlie Francis, *Key Concepts: Elite Edition*;
  Fergus Connolly, *Game Changer*; Kelly Starrett, *Becoming a Supple Leopard*,
  second edition; ALTIS Foundation Course and the cited Clark presentation.
  These are books/course material, not additional peer-reviewed trials. Relevant
  user-provided PDFs/excerpts would enable direct review. Public course/product
  descriptions do not count as reading those works. The article's NESN/Belichick
  citation is an anecdotal quotation, not scientific evidence; not separately
  reviewed. No claim to have reviewed the Clark paper's entire bibliography.

**Crucial accounting distinction, verified from Table 11:**

- Skill acceleration example: 3 rounds × (50 yd power skips + 30 yd push-up-start
  sprint) = 240 yd. Only 90 yd is the final sprint exercise; 150 yd is skips.
  Two medicine-ball throws per round are not included in that distance.
- Skill top-speed example: 2 rounds × (50 yd drop-in acceleration + 40 yd
  dribble-bleed + 20/20/20 yd sprint–float–sprint) = 300 yd. The total includes
  dribbling, run-ins, and float segments, not exclusively maximal-speed running.
- Therefore these totals cannot be compared directly with our acceleration +
  curved + fly-zone subtotal, or relabeled as measured high-speed distance.

Other image context to preserve:

- Table 3's novice/intermediate/top-level 8/10/12 m/s categories are illustrative
  coaching categories, not a diagnosis of this user's level or recovery capacity.
- Table 4 has two sprint high days; Table 5 has three. Josse's Tables 6–7 have

### Implications for this project — interpretation, not new prescriptions

1. **Keep speed present while phase emphasis changes.** This fits the user's
   adopted stress-budget philosophy: lifting leads early; field speed/elasticity,
   COD, and conditioning gain emphasis later while meaningful heavy strength
   remains. It does not require either negligible early sprinting or increases
   in every training category together. Faster intent is not automatically lower
   total stress.
2. **Maintain distinct acceleration and upright-speed objectives.** Preserve
   planned fly work rather than assuming short starts, curves, or warm-up drills
   replace it. Conversely, a longer acceleration can include high-speed running;
   exercise labels are not mutually exclusive physiological exposures. Do not
   assign this user the NFL sample's modeled velocity percentages or assume a
   universal distance at which maximum speed is reached. Fly run-in adequacy
   should be assessed from execution/timing, not inferred from the fly-zone name.
3. **Stable build-week doses are a reasonable project option.** Judge progression
   by execution and timed output as well as distance. This does not establish the
   proposed sets/reps as optimal. Preserve reduced/travel/taper weeks and approved
   rest periods rather than importing a constant-volume rule into every week.
4. **Do not automatically add sprint days.** The present two sprint-bearing days
   sit within a six-day plan with a third jump/gym/conditioning high day. Josse's
   four-day examples have a different recovery structure. Any future frequency
   experiment should explicitly redistribute work and evaluate the whole week;
   keep actual low days low. The user's Achilles history is relevant context,
   not something an illustrative CNS model can clear for higher frequency.
5. **Keep two distance ledgers plus COD/contact context.** Report the main sprint
   prescriptions by type, and separately report fly run-ins, warm-up buildups,
   other moving drills, and known total locomotor distance. If speed thresholds
   are not measured, label high-speed exposure as unknown, not equal to the full
   distance. Unknown COD distances remain unknown; record sets/reps, direction,
   and intent. Jump contacts remain a separate measure, not convertible to meters.
6. **Use evidence of quality to guide additional work.** Standardized timing/video
   can help distinguish improved performance from simply completing more reps.
   Preserve recovery between fast efforts; do not accumulate distance by turning
   speed practice into conditioning. Any stopping threshold would be an explicit
   programming choice, not a validated cutoff supplied by these sources.

Connection to the latest sprint-volume discussion: user proposals and the agent's
clean reduced-week suggestions have **not been implemented by this review** and
must not replace verified workbook values in an audit. The user's proposed normal
P5 week totals 110 m acceleration + 60 m curves + 75 m fly zones = **245 m**.
Adding two existing 200-yd moving warm-ups gives **610.76 m** before fly run-ins,
COD, or other uncounted running. That is a mixed locomotor subtotal, not 610.76 m
of maximal sprinting. It illustrates why comparing 245 m directly with Table 8's
800–1,200 yd is misleading; it does not establish equal training stress.

For the next dosage decision, evaluate how much actual upright fast running the
proposal preserves, not only how much acceleration distance increases. Nothing
in this review supplies an optimal ultimate-specific weekly meter target, proves
that four exposures outperform two for this user, or settles the pending COD plan.
