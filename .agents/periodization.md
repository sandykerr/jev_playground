### User's stress-budget model — 2026-09-26

The user describes each phase as a histogram/bar chart of physical qualities:
**lifts, true plyos, jump training/landings, sprints, conditioning, and COD**.
One or two bars receive more of the overall stress budget than the others;
the intention is not to put every quality at its minimum or maximum together.
The user reports discussing this approach with a trusted trainer who agreed.
This is the user's adopted planning philosophy, not a measured stress model or
independently verified trainer assessment of the detailed program.

- Early phases: lifting accounts for most bodily stress, with smaller amounts
  of plyometrics, jump training/landings, sprinting, and conditioning.
- Toward the season: shift gym work toward higher-intent, lower-fatigue
  selections/doses such as isometrics and velocity-focused lifts, while shifting
  more stress toward elastic/ping-tier plyos and increasing emphasis on sprint
  volume, COD, and conditioning. Retain the previously adopted meaningful heavy
  lifting exposure; this is a redistribution, not removal of strength work.
- Assess each training type in the context of the whole phase: identify the
  leading qualities, the supporting/maintenance qualities, and what makes room
  in the budget when another quality gains emphasis. Do not optimize each bar
  independently or assume every modality should increase together.
- The bars represent relative training stress/emphasis, not directly comparable
  raw counts of sets, contacts, sprint meters, or conditioning minutes. The
  model does not supply numerical stress weights, exact doses, or a requirement
  for uninterrupted week-to-week increases through deloads and the final taper.
  Higher intent and lower fatigue are programming aims, not guarantees from an
  exercise name alone.

Use the user's intent/contact-time taxonomy in [plyos](plyos.md) when discussing
these bars. This context update does not modify workout prescriptions or audit
calculations; the existing final-taper and calendar decisions remain in place.

Develop speed, acceleration and jumping with changing emphasis rather than
removing all other qualities. Build gym structure/capacity early while microdosing
field work; later reduce gym volume, retain meaningful heavy force exposure and
progress speed/jump demands. Jumping needs its own development plan, not just
sprint drills. COD supports ultimate without displacing straight-line/deep-space
priorities.

High/low organization accounts for combined sprint, jump, lifting, conditioning
and sport stress. Normal reviewed week: Tue/Sat sprint high days, Thu submaximal
gym/conditioning, lower-stress days between. Fast intent, heavy load, maximal
effort and whole-session stress differ. High/low is a framework, not a universal
recovery-time rule; accumulating volume can turn a low day into a hard one.

Keep enough work to develop capacity, then protect quality as output rises.
Coaches' in-season minimums are not automatic offseason targets. The early cut
does not justify constraining the entire later build; a surplus does not justify
unlimited workload. Final taper reduces training, not automatically food.
