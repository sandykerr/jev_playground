## Garage Strength: separate roles within an athletic strength program

**Observed in source:** PDF pp. 2–6 describe lower-body power, upper-body power,
an athlete day, an impulse day, and a fifth hypertrophy day. The introduction
frames the fifth day as a bonus and recommends keeping exercise choices for a
four-week block. Sessions distinguish technical coordination, absolute strength,
hypertrophy, plyometrics, trunk control, and what the source calls reflexive strength.

PDF pp. 7–8 offer alternatives within categories. The squat category includes
back, front, and single-leg squats. Lower-body push assistance includes Cossacks,
lunges, and step-ups; pull assistance includes RDL/stiff-leg patterns and Nordics.
Unilateral and bilateral jumps have separate places. Exercise options and rep
schemes are a menu, not instructions to perform every listed option.

**Contribution to this project:** Provides a concrete example of preserving
substantial strength and accessory work while training explosive qualities.
It also supports examining the purpose of lateral and coordinated movements
before calling them redundant. This is relevant to the early-phase volume audit
and the user's preference for purposeful variations.

**Adaptation needed:** Football position benchmarks on PDF p. 9 are source-specific
targets, not requirements for making an ultimate team. The template's lifting
frequency, Olympic-lift emphasis, rest times, and optional fifth session must be
reconciled with actual field work and recovery. Its category names are coaching
labels, not automatically the same as this project's maximal/submaximal labels.

## UNIT Phase 2: explicit loading waves and strength/power separation

**Observed in source:** PDF p. 1 covers weeks 1–4, p. 2 weeks 5–8. Both show
training on Days 1, 2, 4, and 5, with Day 3 off; Days 6–7 are not specified.
Days 1/4 combine Olympic-lift variations, loaded jumps, bounds, and throws.
Days 2/5 emphasize strength work and assistance. Loads commonly rise toward
weeks 3/7, followed by reduced work in weeks 4/8.

The first block pairs front-squat clusters and RDLs on one day, with back squats
and Nordic/GHD work on another. The front-squat notation explicitly specifies
15-second pauses between cluster repetitions. The second block includes above-
parallel box front squats, step-ups, back squats, and Nordic/GHD work. Loaded jumps
change from rhythmic repetitions to discrete/cluster-style efforts. Depth jumps
appear in block 2 and are omitted in week 8.

**Contribution to this project:** Shows why an audit should preserve distinctions
between loaded jumps, strength squats, hinges, and unilateral assistance. Front
squats and RDLs are complementary here. It also supplies an example of decreasing
repetitions while increasing load or jump difficulty: fewer contacts alone do not
establish a lower-stress week.

**Adaptation needed:** Olympic-lifting proficiency is assumed. Some prescriptions
reach maximal efforts or AMRAPs. The reference does not specify a complete ultimate
sprint/conditioning workload. Do not copy its fast-eccentric RDL instruction,
drop-height increases, or maximal tests without evaluating their purpose and fit.
On p. 2, week 8 box front squat refers to a week 3 3RM even though that variation
appears in block 2; preserve this as an unresolved source ambiguity. Do not silently
correct the reference or adopt the ambiguous loading basis.

## Jumping Jacked: strength, accessories, sprinting, and jump practice together

**Observed in source:** PDF p. 5 describes two four-week phases and a suggested
Mon/Wed/Fri/Sat schedule, with recovery days after the two most taxing sessions.
Weeks 4 and 8 have only two listed sessions; week 8 includes maximal strength
testing, so reduced frequency does not mean uniformly low intensity.

Week 1 (PDF pp. 6–9) demonstrates the division of work:

- Day 1: jump practice followed by upper-body work and assistance including hip thrusts.
- Day 2: hops, deep squats, leg extensions, hamstring curls, seated calves, and a bridge.
- Day 3: short sprints, jumps, medicine-ball work, and upper-body training.
- Day 4: band box squats, split squats, RDLs, standing calves, dorsiflexion,
  lateral lunges, and Copenhagen holds.

Later weeks change loads, repetitions, and exercise variants. The source mixes
percentage-based and RPE prescriptions; its lower-body percentages reference deep
squat 1RM, which must not be mistaken for each exercise's own 1RM. Listed squat
sets include ascending loads, so counting every listed set as an equivalent hard
work set would distort comparisons. Sprint distances are in yards.

**Contribution to this project:** Especially relevant to the user's concern that
early phases should build muscular capacity while retaining speed/jump exposure.
It gives concrete precedent for bilateral squats, split squats, RDLs, and targeted
accessories coexisting. The listed split squats do not by themselves establish a
rear-foot-elevated setup; do not relabel them RFESS without checking the demonstration.
The general notes explicitly prioritize recovery between sets over finishing fast.

**Limits:** Jump sessions and warm-ups refer to videos, so the PDF alone cannot
establish total weekly jump contacts. The daily course captures do not fill this
gap. General advice against failure coexists with explicit AMRAP/max-test entries;
read specific prescriptions rather than flattening all sessions to one effort.
Nutrition, sleep, and tendon routines are source advice, not adopted personal
prescriptions. The high upper-body workload reflects the program's muscle-building
goal as well as jumping; it need not be duplicated in an ultimate plan.

## THP: jump-specific planning and technical context

**Observed in source:** PDF pp. 3–11 discuss strength, force-time constraints,
adaptation, and exercise selection. Pages 11–18 discuss plyometrics and distinguish
single efforts, repeated efforts, paused repetitions, loading, intent, and contact
times. Pages 19–29 discuss session sequencing, focused block priorities, fatigue,
retaining previously developed qualities, and reducing load before performance.

Pages 30–51 examine two-foot approach jumping; pp. 52–65 examine one-foot jumping.
Topics include approach speed/rhythm, lowering, penultimate actions, arm timing,
plant/block foot roles, and individual takeoff styles. Pages 66–69 outline the
source's progression from isometrics through slow strength, landing absorption,
and jumping/energy release.

**Contribution to this project:** Treat approach jumping as a technical training
exposure, not merely a generic plyometric contact. It is particularly relevant to
deep-space blocks. The reference encourages inspecting how exercises are executed
and sequenced rather than classifying them solely by names. Contact time, intent,
external load, landing demand, and repeated versus reset execution should remain
distinct in the audit. It also offers a rationale for changing emphasis across
blocks while maintaining other qualities.

**Adaptation needed:** Maximum-height dunking/high-jump practice differs from
tracking a disc, adjusting to an opponent, taking off from an imperfect approach,
and reaching at the right time. Preserve room for those ultimate constraints.
Technical cues are candidates to test against execution and results, not universal
requirements. The guide's terminology and numeric planning rules are its own;
they should not silently redefine the project's existing categories.

The tendon section contains categorical claims and specific rehabilitation doses.
They were read as source content, not independently validated clinical guidance.
Do not use them to diagnose the user, promise pain relief, or dismiss the user's
preferred eccentric work. Any consequential health recommendation needs separate
current evidence and the user's actual symptoms/context. This document records
programming context rather than endorsing all physiological explanations.

  bilateral 5×4 or 4×5. These are choices in a menu, not all to be added together.

  and overall load. Its broad numerical ranges are coach-source guidance,

Interpretation: the user's 1×2–4 medium-tier preparation is not equivalent to
the references' developmental doses. Keep its approved preparation role; judge
developmental bounds/rebounds separately. Recommend planned doses and simple
adjustment rules rather than demanding the user optimize each number. Borrow
progression structure, not the full source workload. Stable reps can accompany
better output; more sets/reps every week is not obligatory. No new dosage wave
was adopted during this reference review.

These are interpretations to inform discussion, not approved tracker changes.

1. **Resolve exercise roles before substitutions.** Across the examples, bilateral
   squats, hinges, unilateral work, and accessories often coexist. This strengthens
   the case for comparing complete sessions when choosing front squat, trap-bar
   deadlift, RFESS, or single-leg RDL for the flexible slots. It does not prove a
   particular choice is best or establish that more volume is needed.
2. **Compare like units.** Separate ramp sets from hard sets; record unilateral
   work per side; preserve cluster structure, tempo, load basis, RPE/RIR, and rest.
   Convert yards to meters explicitly when comparing sprint distances.
3. **Keep jump categories multidimensional.** Loaded jumps, drops without rebound,
   repeated bounds, reset jumps, and approach practice have different purposes.
   An approach-jump tag may overlap a contact-time tag without counting the same
   physical contact twice in the weekly total. Names alone do not establish GCT.
4. **Treat missing doses as unknown.** Video-defined warm-ups and jump sessions
   cannot be logged as zero. Full source workloads are not established where
   those components are unavailable.
5. **Borrow progression logic, then budget total load.** Reconcile gym work with
   acceleration, maximum-speed running, jumps, conditioning, ultimate play,
   calendar constraints, and the active nutrition phase. Do not stack the full
   reference programs onto the current plan.
6. **Use trusted coaches as substantive references.** Explain when a proposal
   follows their examples, when it adapts them, and why ultimate's demands warrant
   the adaptation. Trust is meaningful user context, not proof of universal
   transfer or evidence that a reference prescription is already approved.
