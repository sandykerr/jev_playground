## Main synthesis for this project

1. **Emphasize selected qualities while retaining others.** Ratelle's podcast,
   Blaine's hypertrophy series, and THP's planning chapter support this direction.
   Blaine gives particularly concrete examples of muscle-building priority with
   retained sprinting and explosive lifting. This supports the user's stress-budget
   philosophy without proving that current V6 volume is excessive.
2. **Keep enough work for development.** Launch, Jumping Jacked, UNIT, and Garage
   Strength contain substantial strength/accessory and jump workloads. Coach Q
   explicitly cautions against insufficient preparation. Short in-season examples
   and Ratelle's exercise-count heuristic are not universal offseason ceilings.
3. **Separate physical output from using it in ultimate.** Tobu and Coach Q add
   the clearest treatment of opponent perception, reactive movement, and deliberate
   skill practice. More strength or a faster closed drill does not by itself
   demonstrate better defensive reads or timing a block.
4. **Budget the actual whole week.** Pickup, sprinting, jumps, conditioning and
   lifting all matter. A session called upper body, rehab, tempo or low is not
   automatically easy. Conversely, high intent alone does not establish a high
   total session cost. Source schedules differ substantially.
5. **Measure the intended quality.** Preserve full sprint recovery, jump quality,
   and useful feedback. Distinguish maximal intent from actual output, ramp sets
   from challenging sets, and main sprint distance from run-ins and drills.
   Keep baseline/retest conditions consistent; the new Ratelle transcript
   illustrates how changing run-in or lift-test criteria can inflate gains.
6. **Simplify by purpose, not by deleting every accessory.** For a future V7 review,
   identify each block's primary/secondary goals, each exercise's role, and what
   workload makes room for the priorities. This is an interpretation for future
   discussion, not a new goal pair, volume reduction, or workbook edit.

## Example programs

### Launch — a concrete concurrent lower-body program

Source: [Launch_v1.xlsx](../Diet_Training_Agent_2026_2027/Example_Programs/Launch_v1.xlsx).
Associated with Will Ratelle in the user's source notes; workbook creator metadata
says `Will`. Metadata alone does not independently establish full authorship or
publication date.

`Overview!A1` describes nine weeks, three phases, intermediate athletes, and
lower-body power in several contexts. The ideal schedule is Day 1 Monday, Day 2
Wednesday, Day 3 Friday; tempo Tuesday/Saturday; optional upper Tuesday/Thursday;
Sunday off. A separate layout splits field and gym sessions across days when
same-day training is impractical. Formal sport practice requires schedule management.

| Phase | Observed emphasis and content | Useful project context |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | Varied short starts; build-up sprints; single-leg broads, pogos, Heidens, bounds and skips; squat, RDL, lunges, Nordics, trap-bar DL, unilateral hinges and calves | Substantial gym work coexists with dedicated field/jump work early. It is not a minimal three-exercise-only template. |
| II | Longer/varied starts, single-leg bounds and approach jumps; pin-squat clusters, step-ups, RDLs, isometrics; loaded/unloaded contrasts and a French-contrast day | Progression changes movement and execution as well as dose. Contrast organization is one source option. |
| III | Moving/pogo/supine starts, triple broads, approach practice, explosive step-ups, elevated heavy pulls and overcoming isometrics with reactive jumps | More explosive expression coexists with strength work; later phases are not universally light or low-volume. |

Specific verified examples, useful as reference points rather than proposed doses:

- `Day 1 (I)!B25:U45`: first prescribed week has squat 3×4 at RPE 7–8,
  RDL 3×5, reverse lunge 3×6, Nordic 3×3 and seated calf 2×12, after field work.
- `Day 3 (I)!B17:W20`: build-up sprints are 6/6/8 × 10 yards with a
  15–20-yard build and full rest; bounds and skips have separate yardage.
- `Day 1 (II)!B24:T24`: approach jumps are 10–20 quality reps in each
  populated week. The replacement notes encourage varying takeoff legs/angles.
- `Day 2 (III)!J18:U18`: approach-jump notes progress 10–12, 10–15,
  15–20 minutes; a separate `20` appears in U18. Do not silently convert the
  time prescription into a definitive rep count.
- `Day 2 (III)!B21:U27`: seven rows prescribe elevated trap-bar pulls,
  triples in the first populated week and doubles later, with RPE 7/8 notes.
  This is direct evidence against describing all late Launch lifting as tiny doses.
- `Conditioning!B5:D23`: 60–100-yard tempos at a stated 75% speed, once/twice
  weekly, with 45–60s between reps and 90–120s between sets; reduce/skip when
  sore/fatigued. These are not equivalent to this project's conversational aerobic
  minutes or to maximal sprint meters.

Preserve these source ambiguities:

- Training sheets display four week headers, but workout prescriptions populate
  only the first three. The overview explicitly says nine weeks. Do not count
  blank fourth-week columns as extra scheduled weeks or automatic deloads.
- The workbook distinguishes build-up sprints from its particular definition of
  fly-in sprints: a faster transition from submaximal running, not the same gradual
  build. Do not assume every source uses the project's fly terminology.
- `Day 1 (III)!C35` contains a barbell/DB RPE note alongside power skips over
  hurdles; `Day 3 (III)!J22:T22` repeats a three-rep isometric explanation while
  later rep cells show two. Preserve these as ambiguities before exact copying.
- The nutrition tab's `A2=200` is a template input, not the user's body mass.
  Its weight-loss calorie endpoints both use `10*A2`, while macro endpoints do
  not reconcile to those calories. Do not adopt its formulas as post-cut targets.
- The FAQ's short warm-up suggestion does not supersede the approved project
  sprint warm-up. Its generic claims are not individual preparation requirements.

#### THP — programming general work for athleticism

Source: [program_for_athleticism.txt](../Diet_Training_Agent_2026_2027/Transcripts/Training/thp/program_for_athleticism.txt).

The useful framework separates three questions: where an exercise fits in the
annual plan, its physiological demands, and its mechanical resemblance to the
target action. Means (exercise/implement) and methods (load, sets, rest, time)
jointly determine the stimulus. Repeated cleans with short rests and a few fully
recovered heavy cleans cannot be classified identically from the exercise name.

General work includes bodybuilding, general strength, endurance and jump-endurance
circuits. The coach describes broad general preparation early, more jump-related
general work during middle preparatory blocks, then **less jump-specific general
work near competition** while priority sessions become more specific. Examples
include swimming and constrained general strength late, with jump circuits removed.
Thus not every modality follows the same linear general-to-specific trajectory.

Claims about robustness, recovery and performance gains are the coach's rationale
and athlete anecdotes. The 30-second sand-jump / 30-second rest example supplies
neither a complete progression nor evidence of low tendon load. Count such circuits
as jumping exposure even when called general or low intensity. This framework
helps assess supporting work in the user's stress budget; it supplies no reason
to fill Wednesday rest, add circuits on top of existing work, or finalize Phase 6.
The exercise rankings and motor-unit explanations are source models, not measured
specificity scores or independently verified physiology.

#### THP — individualized strength-to-power sequence

Source: [thp_copy_training_jump_higher.txt](../Diet_Training_Agent_2026_2027/Transcripts/Training/thp/thp_copy_training_jump_higher.txt).

uses approximately four-week mesocycles and blends emphases: submaximal paused
lifts, heavier concentric strength/cleans, maximal rapid-force isometrics and
eventual accentuated eccentric work. Sprint examples extend from 15 m to 30 m,

blocks; the account does not describe removing all heavy strength for a power phase.

The coaches describe managing an athlete's knee symptoms before normal jumping,
belt-squat choices for back symptoms, and lower maximal-isometric volume after a
previous athlete's adverse response. Those anecdotes reinforce individualization;
they do not establish clearance, a rehab timeline, or suitable loads for this user.
Maximal isometrics can be substantial work and should not automatically receive a
low-fatigue label. Their avoiding sprints the day after a particular jump session
is a source scheduling choice, not a universal prohibition on adjacent high days.

The speaker corrects himself while navigating copied/moved calendar entries and
realizes another isometric cycle is present. Exact set/percentage fragments,
theoretical force-velocity sketches and unclear velocity units do not support
rebuilding a reliable weekly spreadsheet. Preserve the broad sequence as a case
study, not a complete reusable template or this project's P1–P6 mapping.

### Training podcast

Source: [Tobu Fitness Training Podcast](<../Diet_Training_Agent_2026_2027/Transcripts/Ultimate-Specific/Tobu Fitness Training Podcast.md>).

The supplied account prioritizes the athlete's actual limitation rather than a
fixed skill/physical-training ratio. It distinguishes frequent deliberate skill
practice from physical training that requires overload and recovery, and treats

Examples include catching after a sprint, decelerating into a throw, faster pivots

distinct from always trying to maximize immediate practice performance.

Quantities such as weekly high-intensity sprint/cut exposure, 3–8 sprint reps,
